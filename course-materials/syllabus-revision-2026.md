Creative Game Development and Design introduces students to foundational concepts and workflows in contemporary video game design and development. Through a series of scaffolded mini-projects and a final project, students will explore core game experience design approaches, including prototyping, iteration, interactive narrative, and audiovisual expression.

The course emphasizes **process over polish** and encourages students to develop playable prototypes through structured experimentation, teamwork, and critical reflection. While the course is studio-based and project-driven, assignments are designed with a **balanced and sustainable workload** appropriate for a 4-credit course.

This course is organized around a four-step design method that runs through every project:

**[Figure: Four-Step Design & Development Cycle — see attached diagram `four-step-cycle.png`]**

In the age of AI Agents, AI tools are treated as **design partners, programming assistants, and asset factories** — not shortcuts that replace thinking. Students learn to describe needs to AI, critically evaluate its output, and understand and modify what it produces. The course requires students to have a basic knowledge of any programming language; C# is taught from the basics inside the engine.

## Course Overview and Learning Outcomes

Many of us have played and enjoyed games. Have you ever wondered how people actually design and develop them? Can games as a profession and practice be described? What are the basic theories, structures, and skills involved in game development? How do game designers create compelling interactive experiences for the player? How do they respond to feedback, prototype, and improve these experiences?

This course explores these questions and others through playing, analyzing, and making games over 14 classes. Students will understand games not only as entertainment, production, and business models but as a form of mass interactive media and culture.

Students will be introduced to game design concepts, emphasizing all stages of game development: paper and digital prototyping, iteration, interactive narrative design, object-oriented game programming, 2D/3D game art creation, sound effects composition, and user testing.

Rather than building five disconnected games, the course is structured as **three independent mini-projects of increasing technical depth** — a paper/digital framework game, a single-player engine world, and a multiplayer game — each following the same four-step method. The final project consolidates the skills into one polished, playable creation.

### Upon completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Understand fundamental principles of game design and interactive experience creation
- Apply the four-step "from experience to game" method to translate a feeling or a domain of knowledge into a playable system
- Build playable games in Tuanjie Engine (团结引擎, the China-localized engine based on Unity 2022 LTS) using C#
- Use AI tools as design partners, programming assistants, and asset generators — and critically evaluate their output
- Apply iterative design methods through testing, feedback, and refinement
- Analyze games as cultural, aesthetic, and experiential artifacts
- Communicate design ideas through playable builds, presentations, and documentation
- Collaborate effectively in small teams on creative projects

### Mini Projects

Students will complete **three mini-projects**, each focusing on a specific aspect of game design and development. These projects are **scaffolded**, increasing in conceptual and technical complexity over time. Each project is **independent** (different game, different skills), but all follow the same four-step method.

#### Mini Project 1: Game Framework through Guided Design (Game One)

- Translate a domain of knowledge or a real-life/learning experience into a playable game framework
- Uses a **guided-questioning** workflow (the same flow as the summer intensive): a series of structured questions systematically turn an experience into mechanics
- **No coding required** — the prototype is paper-based or workshop-based
- Focus on abstraction: identifying the core actions, process, space, and data of the experience
- Scope is intentionally limited; emphasis on design thinking rather than production

#### Mini Project 2: A Game World in the Engine (Game Two)

- Build a single-player, playable game world in Tuanjie Engine (团结引擎)
- Focus on translating the data-abstracted system into C# mechanics, player control, feedback, and game feel
- Students implement a focused, coherent experience rather than a large content set
- Introduces engine fundamentals: editor, GameObjects/Components, physics, UI, and data visualization

#### Mini Project 3: A Multiplayer Game (Game Three)

- Observe social and multiplayer mechanics through a Board Club field trip
- Validate multiplayer rules with a **physical (tabletop) prototype** before going digital
- Implement a playable networked game in Tuanjie Engine
- Focus on system architecture, networking, social presence, and (optionally) AI-driven NPCs / dialogue

### Project Documentation

Documentation supports critical reflection on the design and development process. Requirements are **scaled to match project scope** and must include an **AI collaboration log** (see AI policy below).

- **Mini Project 1:**
  - Paper/digital prototype (photo or playable link)
  - Brief system explanation (the data-abstracted system)
  - AI collaboration log (which AI tools were used, how they were prompted, what was accepted/rejected)
- **Mini Projects 2 & 3:**
  - Gameplay recording
  - Selected design or technical breakdowns (including system architecture diagram)
  - Short reflective writing
  - AI collaboration log
- **Final Project:**
  - Comprehensive documentation, including design motivation, system overview, iteration process, user feedback, and AI collaboration log

Documentation focuses on **clarity and reflection**, not length.

### Presentations

In this course, each group of students will have presentations to demonstrate their game designs. In this session, students not only demonstrate the design results but also invite other groups to test their designs. Even students and related persons outside of the course will participate in the presentation and testing session to give evaluations.

### Use of Generative AI Tools

In this course, generative AI tools are **essential skills to learn and use**, not merely permitted. They serve as design partners, programming assistants, and asset factories across all three projects (Shneiderman 2022; Kantosalo & Toivonen 2016).

**Permitted and Encouraged Uses**
- **Design phase:** brainstorming, guided questioning, rule testing, design critique (e.g., ChatGPT / Claude)
- **Development phase:** code generation and assistance (e.g., GitHub Copilot / Cursor), asset generation for art, audio, and (optionally) NPC dialogue (e.g., MidJourney, Stable Diffusion, Stable Audio, Suno) — see Mateas & Stern 2005; Yannakakis & Togelius 2018; Gallotta et al. 2024
- **Iteration phase:** analyzing playtest feedback and generating improvement suggestions

**Requirements**
1. **Document AI usage.** Every project documentation must include an **AI collaboration log** recording:
   - Which AI tools were used
   - How they were prompted
   - Which outputs were accepted, rejected, or modified, and why
2. **Critical evaluation.** You must demonstrate the ability to evaluate AI-generated content, modify it to fit your vision, and reject suggestions when appropriate (Shneiderman 2022). Submitting an AI-generated artifact without understanding or reflection does not meet the learning objectives.
3. **Prohibited uses.**
   - Using AI to generate your reflective writing or reading responses and presenting them as your own thinking
   - Using AI to bypass the learning objectives of a project

**Ethical considerations.** We will discuss copyright and ownership of AI-generated content, bias in AI tools, and the role of human creativity in AI-assisted work (see also Sicart 2014 on the ethics/politics of design).

*Key readings underpinning this policy:* see **AI & Generative Tools** in Assigned Readings (Shneiderman 2022; Kantosalo & Toivonen 2016; Gallotta et al. 2024).

**Library Services**

The Library is available to support your research needs. They have access to over 27,000 print resources, 2,000 DVDs, and 1,000 databases (including over a million e-books, as well as streaming audio and video and image databases).

Librarians with expertise in your research topic are available to meet either *in person* or *online* [by appointment](https://nyu-shanghai.libcal.com/appointments) or [by email](https://nyu.qualtrics.com/jfe/form/SV_893SmPggYfKqOz3?Q_JFE=qdg) to help you navigate the research process. Our library team features experts in Business, Arts & Humanities, STEM, Social Sciences & Economics, and data tools & resources. Ask us how we can assist you in developing a research question and formulating a research strategy, to selecting databases, requesting materials, and citing your sources. Visit [shanghai.nyu.edu/library](http://shanghai.nyu.edu/library) for more information on:

- 24/7 access to e-books, e-journals, streaming media, and databases
- Booking one-on-one consultations for research help
- [Asking the Library](https://shanghai.nyu.edu/academics/library/services/aal) questions via chat or email

**Electronic Reserves**

Students can access course readings using their NYU credentials for courses they currently enrolled in at [https://ares.library.nyu.edu/](https://ares.library.nyu.edu/).

**Interlibrary Loan Service**

For materials not available to you immediately, you can request scanned copies of a book chapter or journal article through our [Interlibrary Loan (ILL) service](https://ill.library.nyu.edu/). If you don't know which chapter you need, you can request a Table of Content through ILL.
